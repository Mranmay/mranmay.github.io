The bit of convergent cross mapping (CCM) that took me a while to get was the direction. If $X$ drives $Y$, why would I try to estimate $X$ from $Y$? Sugihara and colleagues’ idea was that the response can carry a record of what drove it. A good place to look for a signal from $X$, then, is in the recent history of $Y$. I wanted to see that happen in a system where I already knew the answer. [Sugihara et al., *Detecting Causality in Complex Ecosystems*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1227079)

I got the idea for the particular simulation from a different paper. Amin Nejatbakhsh, Luca Mazzucato and colleagues tested their reconstruction method on a recurrent network with a subsystem governed by Rössler dynamics. They also studied real recordings and microstimulation in macaque prefrontal cortex. I borrowed the oscillator-and-network setup for a smaller exercise of my own. All the traces below are simulated. [Nejatbakhsh et al., *Physical Review Research*](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.5.043211)

## The connection I knew was there

I had a Rössler oscillator drive a 100-unit recurrent rate network. Then I threw away nearly everything. I kept just the oscillator’s first coordinate and the activity of the network’s fourth unit. Could a short history of that one unit tell me what the oscillator had been doing?

At first glance the blue curve looks encouraging. Then I read the vertical axis. Its best correlation is under **0.04**, and giving it more examples sometimes makes it worse. If someone had handed me just these curves, I couldn’t have told them with any confidence how the network was wired.

## Going back to the oscillator

To check my calculation, I took the network out and kept the oscillator’s three coordinates. Its equations are

$$
\dot{x}_1=-x_2-x_3,\qquad
\dot{x}_2=x_1+a x_2,\qquad
\dot{x}_3=b+x_3(x_1-c).
$$

Suppose I cover up $x_1$ and let myself see only $x_2$. One value of $x_2$ is ambiguous. The oscillator may have passed that value on the way up or on the way down. So for each time $t$, I write down $x_2$ now, five samples ago and ten samples ago. Those three numbers give me a little history I can look up.

I keep the first $L$ histories in a library alongside the value $x_1$ had at the end of each one. For each later history, I find its four closest matches, look up their $x_1$ values and average them, giving the closest matches more weight. Since I still have the simulated $x_1$ trace, I can uncover it and check my estimates. Swapping the two coordinates lets me run the same check in reverse.

<CcmReconstruction />

At 50 library points I could reconstruct $x_1$ from the history of $x_2$ with a correlation of about **0.56**. Swapping the coordinates gave only **0.18**. Select 185 points and the gap almost disappears. Each point scores all estimates after that library against the simulated coordinate at the same times. The two directions use the same test times at each library size. As the library grows, the remaining test period gets shorter.

I used $a=b=0.2$ and $c=5.7$, starting at $(1,0,0)$. I ran 15,000 Euler steps of length 0.05 and discarded the first 2,000.

That comparison helped me understand “convergent.” With 50 examples, some stretches of the orbit have poor matches in my library. With a few hundred, the oscillator has revisited enough nearby states that I can recover either coordinate well. A correlation close to one in both directions makes sense here. I’m watching two coordinates of the same system.

What stuck with me was how differently the very same question worked out for one unit in the network. I can build in a real input and still see almost no sign of it in the two traces I chose. With actual neurons I’d have another complication. Two cells watching the same moving grating might predict each other because both respond to the stimulus. I’d want to see what survived after matching the stimulus across trials and checking the time lag before drawing a circuit diagram from those curves. [Cross mapping with time lags](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep14750)
