I was looking into how people study causality in neuroscience. Say I record from two sites in cortex and their activity goes up and down together. Can I use those recordings to work out whether stimulating one site would do anything to the other? Training a large neural network can get expensive, and I wanted to see how far I could get with the time series themselves.

Granger causality seemed like a good place to start. Suppose I want to predict what happens next at site B. I make one prediction using B’s recent activity, then try again with A’s recent activity available too. If adding A consistently improves the forecast on new data, A carries information about B’s future beyond what B’s own past tells me. The usual linear version is cheap to fit. One reason to look further is that the effect of an input might depend on what the circuit is already doing, which a simple linear model can miss. [Granger’s original paper](https://doi.org/10.2307/1912791)

I came across convergent cross mapping (CCM) in a paper by Amin Nejatbakhsh, Luca Mazzucato and colleagues. They recorded spontaneous activity in macaque prefrontal cortex and used it to predict what would happen when they electrically stimulated individual sites. I liked that they had a way to check the answer. Their CCM-based measure did better than the Granger measures they tested at predicting those stimulation effects. That’s the comparison that got me interested. [Nejatbakhsh et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.5.043211)

The idea originally came from Sugihara and colleagues, who were studying ecological time series. The direction took me a minute. With Granger I’d used A’s past to help predict B’s future. With CCM, if I think A drives B, I look in **B’s history for information about A**. If the two are coupled, similar stretches of B’s trajectory can correspond to similar states of A. Looking up nearby stretches lets me pick up a nonlinear relationship without having to guess its equation. [Sugihara et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1227079)

## Trying it on a connection I put there myself

I borrowed the Rössler oscillator idea from the neuroscience paper. In my simulation the oscillator drove a network of 100 recurrent units. I kept two traces, the oscillator’s first coordinate and the activity of the fourth network unit, and tried the reconstruction in both directions. I expected that knowing the wiring would make the plot fairly easy to read.

The blue line does sit above the orange one. The problem is the scale. Even at its best the correlation is below 0.04. I knew this unit belonged to a network receiving input from the oscillator, but I’d have had a hard time arguing that from these traces. So I took the network out and tried a simpler check.

## Could I recover one coordinate from another?

This time I kept two coordinates of the oscillator itself. I hid $x_1$ and wrote down three values of $x_2$ at each step, the current value and the values five and ten samples earlier. That little bit of history matters. The oscillator passes through the same height on its way up and on its way down.

I used the first 50 histories as a reference library. For a later stretch of $x_2$, I looked up the four closest matches in that library. I knew what $x_1$ had been at each of those four times, so I averaged those values, giving more weight to closer matches. Then I uncovered $x_1$ to see how well I’d done.

<CcmReconstruction />

At 50 points my estimates correlated about **0.56** with the actual $x_1$. Swapping the coordinates gave **0.18**. Try 185 points in the figure. Both correlations are now above **0.95**. That made the “convergent” part click for me. With a longer stretch of the orbit to search, I had a much better chance of finding a close match. Both directions worked here because I was observing two coordinates of the same low-dimensional system.

For this check I used the standard Rössler parameters $a=b=0.2$ and $c=5.7$. I started at $(1,0,0)$, took 15,000 Euler steps of length 0.05 and dropped the first 2,000. Each score uses the samples after its reference library, so the two directions are compared over the same times at each library size.

## Where I’d use it in neuroscience

I’d use this to decide which connections to test experimentally. In visual cortex, for instance, two cells can both respond to the same moving grating. Before getting excited about a high cross-map score, I’d want to know whether it held up across repeated presentations of that grating and whether the timing made sense for the proposed direction of influence.

What I like about the macaque experiment is that it takes the next step. You can pick a site using activity you’ve already recorded, say where else you expect a response, then stimulate and see what you got right. My little network example also gives me a reason to keep some low-scoring pairs in that experiment. I put the input there myself and still barely saw it.
